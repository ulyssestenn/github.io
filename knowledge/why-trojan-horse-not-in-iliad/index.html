Why Is the Trojan Horse Not in the Iliad?

The most famous object in the Trojan War belongs to a part of the story the Iliad never reaches.

Nico

The Trojan Horse belongs to the Trojan War, but not to the stretch of the story covered by the Iliad. Modern retellings usually compress the whole war into one continuous sequence: Paris takes Helen, the Greeks sail to Troy, Achilles fights Hector, the wooden horse enters the city, and Troy burns. That compression is useful, but it gives first-time readers a misleading sense of what Homer's poem is trying to do.

The Iliad covers only a small part of the war

When the poem opens, the Greeks have already been at Troy for nine years. Its subject is the anger of Achilles, beginning with his quarrel with Agamemnon and moving through the death of Patroclus, Achilles' return to battle, the killing of Hector, and Priam's visit to Achilles to recover his son's body.

By the final scene, Hector is being buried. Troy is still standing; Achilles and Paris are still alive; the Horse has not been built. The poem makes no attempt to bridge the remaining events of the war. It has selected one crisis from a much larger legendary story.

The Horse appears elsewhere in Homeric poetry

If by “Homer” we mean the Iliad and Odyssey, the Horse is already part of the world of the poems. In Book 4 of the Odyssey, Menelaus remembers sitting inside it with the other Greek warriors while Helen circles the structure and calls out in the voices of their wives. Odysseus stops the men from answering. The Center for Hellenic Studies discusses and translates the passage here.

Book 8 is more explicit. Odysseus asks the bard Demodocus to sing of the wooden horse made by Epeius with Athena's help, filled with Greek warriors and brought into Troy as a stratagem. That episode survives in the Odyssey itself. These passages place the Horse inside the traditional story familiar to the poems; its absence from the Iliad tells us where that poem chooses to stop.

Ancient readers had more Trojan War poetry than we do

We happen to possess the Iliad and Odyssey complete, while other early Greek epics about Troy survive only in fragments and summaries. The lost poems of the Epic Cycle filled in much of the narrative around Homer.

A surviving summary attributed to Proclus tells us that the Little Iliad included Epeius constructing the Horse, the best Greek warriors entering it, and the rest of the army sailing away to Tenedos. The following Iliou Persis, or Destruction of Ilion, carried the story through the debate over the Horse and the sack of Troy. The Center for Hellenic Studies publishes the surviving summaries. An ancient audience therefore encountered the Trojan War as a body of stories much larger than any one poem.

Centuries later, many of the details that dominate the modern imagination received their most famous surviving treatment in Book 2 of Vergil's Aeneid: Sinon, Laocoön's warning, the serpents, the Trojans hauling the Horse into the city, and the Greeks emerging at night. Vergil's account is here. He inherited the Horse from an old Greek tradition and gave that part of the story a Roman masterpiece of a telling.

The omission tells you what kind of poem the Iliad is

The Iliad could have carried its story forward to Troy's destruction, but doing so would have changed its center of gravity. Instead, it ends after Priam enters the enemy camp, begs Achilles for Hector's body, and sits with the man who killed his son. The funeral that follows leaves Troy standing, although the war's outcome is never in doubt. Hector has already foreseen the city's destruction before his death; Andromache has imagined what its fall will mean; the audience knows the larger story.

That foreknowledge lets the poem stop where its own subject has reached completion. The Horse belongs to what happens next. The Iliad has spent its attention on Achilles' rage and on what finally interrupts the brutal logic of that rage: mortality, honor, pity, and the moment when Achilles is forced to recognize another grieving human being in front of him.
