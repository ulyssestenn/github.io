Why Read More Than One Translation of the Same Classic?

The second translation is not useful because the first one failed. It is useful because translation necessarily makes choices that become easier to see by comparison.

Nico

Reading two translations is one of the easiest ways to discover where the original text stops and the translator begins.

This does not mean that every reader should read the same book twice from cover to cover. Often a few pages, a favorite scene, or a difficult passage is enough.

The point is comparison.

No translation is transparent

Languages divide experience differently. They arrange sentences differently. Their words have different ranges of meaning, histories, registers, rhythms, and associations.

A translator therefore cannot simply replace each word with its English equivalent and leave everything else untouched. Even when a literal equivalent exists, the sentence may require decisions about order, emphasis, tone, repetition, ambiguity, and sound.

Poetry makes the problem especially obvious because form is part of the meaning. Rhyme, meter, line length, repeated formulas, wordplay, and sound cannot all be preserved at full strength while every semantic detail also stays in the same place.

A translator is always deciding what must survive most clearly.

Comparison reveals hidden decisions

Read one translation alone and a sentence can seem inevitable. Put another translation beside it and inevitability disappears.

One translator preserves a repeated epithet while another varies it. One keeps an ambiguous word ambiguous while another chooses a meaning. One uses an old familiar name, another a Greek-looking transliteration. One writes prose, another verse. One preserves an abrupt sentence, another smooths it into idiomatic English.

None of this automatically tells you which translation is “right.” It tells you where the interesting question is.

That is already a major gain.

Older translations are not merely obsolete versions

A translation is also a historical document.

Alexander Pope's Homer tells us something about Homer, but it also tells us what eighteenth-century English poetry could sound like at its most accomplished. George Chapman's Homer belongs to another English literary world. Samuel Butler's prose versions reflect yet another judgment about what a modern reader needs from an ancient epic.

The older translation is therefore not simply waiting to be replaced by the newest one.

Sometimes a newer translation is more accurate on a particular point. Sometimes an older translation is less useful as a crib but more interesting as English literature. Sometimes the older version preserves a feature another translator smooths away. Sometimes it introduces a feature the Greek never had.

The differences are the reason to compare them.

Do not turn comparison into shopping

There is a slightly absurd version of translation comparison in which a reader spends weeks selecting the perfect edition and never actually reads Homer.

That defeats the purpose.

For a first read, choose a translation that keeps you reading. Samuel Butler's prose is an excellent public-domain starting point for someone who wants direct access to the story. If a passage catches your attention, compare it with Pope, Chapman, or a modern translation. If you know some Greek, put the original beside them.

The comparison should deepen reading, not postpone it.

Some passages repay comparison more than others

You do not need to compare every sentence. Look for places where translation pressure is high.

  • Famous openings. Translators announce their priorities quickly.
  • Repeated formulas and epithets. Does the English preserve or disguise the recurrence?
  • Wordplay or ambiguity. One English phrase may have to choose where the original does not.
  • Highly emotional speeches. Register matters enormously.
  • Violence, sex, obscenity, or insult. Translators and eras differ in what they soften or sharpen.
  • Meter-heavy passages. Sound may force choices that disappear in prose.

When translations diverge sharply, that is not necessarily a defect. It may be evidence that the original has given the translator a genuinely difficult problem.

A translation can be excellent without being definitive

This is perhaps the most useful habit to acquire.

A reader can admire a translation without pretending it has abolished the need for all others. The better a work is, the more likely it is that different translators can uncover different things in it.

A plain prose translation may reveal structure. A formally ambitious verse translation may restore energy or sound. A scholarly translation may preserve distinctions that a more literary version compresses. An old translation may show the history of the work's reception in English.

There is no contradiction in preferring one while learning from another.

The second translation changes how you read the first

After comparison, you stop seeing translated prose as a pane of glass.

You begin noticing naming policy, sentence shape, repetition, register, rhythm, and interpretive confidence. You start asking whether a smooth phrase was smooth in the original, whether a strange phrase was strange there too, and whether a beautiful English line belongs primarily to the ancient poet or to the translator.

That slight loss of innocence is useful.

Translation becomes visible as an art of judgment rather than a clerical operation. The original work becomes larger because you can see more than one route through it.

Breaker of Horses
