Saturn Has Grown a Decagon at Its South Pole

A new ten-sided wave may let astronomers watch a planetary-scale atmospheric polygon develop.

Annie

Saturn's north pole has had a hexagon for as long as spacecraft have been able to inspect it closely. Now the south pole has produced something stranger: a ten-sided atmospheric wave that seems to have appeared within the past decade.

Cassini saw no comparable long-lived polygon before its mission ended in 2017. Hubble data show weak traces of the southern pattern beginning in 2023, followed by a much clearer decagon. The important point is not merely that astronomers finally got a good picture. They may have caught a planetary-scale atmospheric structure while it is developing.

Ten sides, each wider than Earth

The scale is almost impolite. The decagon spans about 168,000 kilometers across. One side is roughly 16,800 kilometers long. Earth's diameter is about 12,700 kilometers.

The researchers interpret the shape as a large meandering wave associated with a powerful eastward jet stream. The jet moves at about 116 meters per second. The polygonal wave drifts much more slowly, about 2.5 meters per second, taking roughly 800 days to circle the planet.

That makes it unlike Saturn's famous northern hexagon, which has remained nearly stationary and recognizable for more than four decades. The southern decagon moves and appears to be evolving. It may not be a southern copy of the hexagon at all.

How do you know a feature is new?

Saturn's seasons make that harder than it sounds. The planet takes about 29.5 Earth years to orbit the Sun, and for years the south polar region was poorly placed for observation from Earth. The researchers therefore cannot say exactly when the decagon formed. The defensible window is between Cassini's last observations in 2017 and Hubble's detection in 2023.

Ground-based observers noticed an undulating southern band in 2024, with stronger evidence in 2025. Hubble then supplied sharper repeated observations and let the team trace weaker signatures backward into 2023 data. NASA's long-running OPAL program matters here precisely because comparable observations across years can reveal a change that a single exquisite image cannot.

Different wavelengths see different Saturns

Hubble observed the feature through filters that probe different atmospheric altitudes. The apparent position shifts slightly among them, evidence that the wave extends vertically rather than merely marking a superficial cloud boundary.

The team's models show that a meandering wave trapped by the curvature of the jet can produce a polygonal pattern. Possible triggers include a periodic disturbance in the jet and a nearby anticyclonic vortex. Those are explanations under test, not a solved mechanism.

NASA's Astronomy Picture of the Day featured the decagon on September 8. The timing is unusually good: the feature is strengthening while Saturn's southern hemisphere becomes easier to observe.

The hexagon is now a comparison group

For decades Saturn's north-polar hexagon was a sample size of one. Now researchers can compare two large polygonal waves on the same planet: six sides versus ten, nearly stationary versus migrating, apparently long-lived versus still evolving, north versus south.

That may be more scientifically useful than simply finding another geometric curiosity. Hubble, Webb, ground-based telescopes, and atmospheric models can now test whether the decagon settles into a durable structure, changes its geometry, or breaks apart.

The remarkable fact is not merely that Saturn can make a decagon. We may have arrived in time to watch what happens next.
