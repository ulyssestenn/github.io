Good Notes May Work Because They Show You What You Don't Understand

A new experiment suggests that useful notes may improve learning partly by making overconfidence harder to maintain.

Donald

Cal Newport's recent video Should I Use Notebooks More Often? (Cal's Strategy) begins with a familiar failure mode. A nice notebook is purchased with excellent intentions and then sits there because nobody decided what the notebook was actually for.

Newport's answer is to give different notebooks specific jobs. That is practical advice drawn from his own workflow, not a learning experiment. But a new study in Learning and Instruction makes the underlying question more interesting: when notes do help someone learn, what is the useful part of the activity?

The answer may have less to do with producing an impressive set of notes than with making it harder to remain comfortably mistaken about what you understand.

The experiment changed the job of the notes

Mengsi Liu and Yuri Uesaka randomly assigned 94 high-school students to four conditions while they learned mathematics from recorded lessons. The study varied two things: whether students received a short lesson in what the researchers call deep notetaking, and whether they wrote in a separate notebook or directly in the textbook.

The deep-notetaking instruction was not elaborate. It directed students to pay attention to the meaning of the material, solutions to problems, and points they did not understand well. In other words, the notes were supposed to do more than preserve what had appeared on the screen. They were supposed to make the student process and monitor it.

That instruction produced better scores on the mathematics knowledge post-test, with a modest effect size of partial η² = .07. Whether students wrote in a notebook or directly in the textbook did not have a significant main effect, and there was no significant interaction between medium and the deeper strategy.

It would be easy to turn that into a fight about notebooks. The study does not support one. Both media in the experiment were physical, so it tells us nothing decisive about handwriting versus typing or paper versus digital notes.

What it does suggest is more useful: the cognitive job assigned to the notes mattered.

The notes may have interrupted false confidence

The most interesting result came from an exploratory analysis of why the deep-notetaking group learned more.

The researchers measured students' monitoring bias: the gap between how well students thought they understood the material and how well they actually performed. Deep-notetaking instruction reduced the tendency to overestimate understanding. In the control condition, 34.8 percent of students were classified as overestimating; with deep-notetaking instruction, that fell to 22.7 percent.

There is an important wrinkle. The intervention did not simply make everybody's self-assessment more accurate. Underestimation increased, from 23.9 percent to 38.6 percent. The proportion classified as accurate was roughly similar. The shift was toward more conservative judgment, not perfect calibration.

Still, the mediation analysis linked the reduction in overestimation to higher post-test scores. Measured note quality, by contrast, did not significantly mediate the effect.

That is a useful result because it changes what we might look for in a learning notebook. A page can be incomplete and still have done valuable cognitive work. A beautifully complete page can coexist with the conviction that familiar-looking material has been mastered when it has not.

Trying to explain what something means, connect it to what came before, or mark the step that still does not make sense can expose that difference while there is still time to do something about it.

A note can be useful before you ever reread it

Notes have at least two different lives.

One is external. A note preserves something for later: a quotation, a page reference, a formula, an argument, a question, a source. This is the obvious function of notebooks and personal knowledge systems, and it matters.

The other happens during the act of making the note. Selecting an idea, putting it into one's own structure, working through a solution, or admitting “I don't understand why this step follows” can change the learner's relationship to the material before the note is ever retrieved.

The Liu and Uesaka study is narrow. It involved 94 high-school students learning mathematics, and the proposed mediating mechanism comes from exploratory analysis. It would be a mistake to turn it into a general law that everyone should take deep notes on everything.

It would also be a mistake to miss the practical implication because the result is not universal.

If the goal is merely to preserve a passage, preserving it may be enough. If the goal is to learn something, a useful note can have a slightly harder job. Instead of recording only what the source said, try adding one of the things the study forced students to confront: what it means, how the reasoning works, or what still does not make sense.

That gives the notebook a job. More importantly, it gives the learner a way to discover that understanding has not happened yet.

Relevant Ulix tools

Guten and Keep Clip

Guten lets you highlight passages, attach notes, add bookmarks, and search while reading public-domain books offline. If a passage is something you want to learn rather than simply find again, use the note for a quick explanation, connection, or question instead of duplicating the sentence you just highlighted.

Keep Clip is a focused Android capture tool for notes, quotes, highlights, excerpts, and links from almost any app. It is useful when the important passage is outside your book reader and you want to keep the text and the reason it mattered.

Neither app turns note-taking into a learning intervention by itself. The useful distinction is between saving something for later retrieval and doing enough thinking at capture time to discover whether you actually understand it.
