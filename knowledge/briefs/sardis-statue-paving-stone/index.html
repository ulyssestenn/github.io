The 2,500-Year-Old Statue That Became a Paving Stone

A statue carved in fifth-century BCE Sardis combined contemporary anatomy with an older Lydian visual language. Centuries later, Roman builders treated it as stone.

Nico

At some point in Roman Sardis, somebody needed paving material. A marble statue of a young man, tall enough that the complete figure would have stood well over seven feet, had become useful stone.

The statue was broken into two large pieces and laid face down in a colonnaded avenue. In late May 2026, archaeologists with the Harvard-Cornell Archaeological Exploration of Sardis found it there. The strange part is not only that a monumental sculpture ended up underfoot. The statue seems to have been looking backward even when it was new.

Its upright stance, carefully arranged long hair, and clothing recall the archaic sculpture of the sixth century BCE. If those were the only clues, the statue might look a century older than it probably is. Other details point to the 470s or 450s BCE. The body is observed more closely, down to prominent veins, in a way that belongs much more comfortably to the early Classical period.

Sardis at that time was no longer the capital of an independent Lydian kingdom. Cyrus the Great had conquered Lydia in the sixth century, and the city was under Achaemenid Persian rule. Yet Nicholas Cahill, the excavation's field director, notes that the new figure shows little obvious interest in Persian court style. Its combination is different: newer Greek anatomical knowledge joined to older forms associated with Lydia and western Anatolia.

Why does a fifth-century statue look older than it is?

There is a tempting but unhelpful way to describe art history as a sequence of improvements. Archaic stiffness gives way to Classical naturalism; one style becomes obsolete and another replaces it. Sardis does not cooperate very well with that story.

The sculpture already known from the site shows why. George Hanfmann's catalogue of Lydian and Persian-period sculpture describes Sardis as a place where Mediterranean and Near Eastern traditions met. Eastern Greek influence could be strong, while local dress, working methods, iconography, and deliberately conservative forms persisted. By the middle of the fifth century, Sardian marble technique could be difficult to distinguish from Greek work even when the object itself did not simply become Greek.

The newly found statue makes that coexistence especially easy to see. Its sculptor seems to have known what contemporary bodies looked like in stone and chosen not to discard an older visual language. The old-fashioned elements therefore matter as choices. They may tell us something about how Lydians living under Persian rule imagined continuity with their own past.

We do not yet know whom the statue represented or where it originally stood. Harvard curator Susanne Ebbinghaus has raised the lost sanctuary of Cybele as one possibility: literary sources describe it as an important sanctuary at Sardis, and elite worshippers sometimes placed their likenesses near a deity. The sanctuary itself has never been found, so this remains a hypothesis rather than an identification.

Then it stopped being a statue

Its later life is easier to see and harder to interpret. Someone broke it, turned the pieces over, and incorporated them into a street.

It would be satisfying to turn that into a story of cultural contempt: Romans conquer a place, despise its old gods or heroes, and walk over them. There is no need to invent that motive. Ancient builders reused stone constantly, and Sardis supplies strong evidence of the habit. Earlier excavations found archaic sculpture built into Roman walls, classical reliefs laid face down in later architecture, a carved lion incorporated into a synagogue pier, and old stelai recycled into masonry.

Archaeologists use the word spolia for older architectural or sculptural material reused in later construction. Sometimes reuse was conspicuously symbolic. Sometimes an excellent block of marble was an excellent block of marble. Without better evidence, the paving stones do not tell us which attitude the builders had toward the young man they dismantled.

They do tell us that the object's category had changed. A figure made to be seen had become a material with a flat side, a useful thickness, and enough strength to survive traffic.

Face-down reuse also protected surfaces that might otherwise have weathered or been recut. Conservators are now cleaning the fragments while preserving surviving flecks of pigment for color analysis. When the work is complete, the pieces are expected to be joined for display in a Turkish archaeological museum.
