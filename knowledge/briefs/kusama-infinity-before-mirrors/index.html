Kusama's Infinity Rooms Did Not Begin With Mirrors

Before mirrors multiplied Kusama's rooms, she spent years painting and sewing repetition by hand.

Nico

The mirrors arrived late.

Yayoi Kusama, whose death at 97 was announced this week, is now so closely associated with Infinity Mirror Rooms that the optical device can look like the idea itself. A small room, mirrored walls, repeated lights or objects, and suddenly the architecture seems to go on forever.

Kusama had been trying to make repetition escape the boundaries of an artwork for years before she put mirrors on the walls.

When she arrived in New York in 1958, she had already developed what she called the Infinity Net. The paintings are built from innumerable small loops or semicircular marks spread across large canvases. There is no obvious focal point and no privileged patch of surface. The eye can enter almost anywhere and find more of the same activity continuing beyond it.

That matters because the paintings emerged in a New York art world still dominated by the large gesture. A brushstroke could advertise the artist's movement across a canvas. Kusama's mark was almost perversely modest by comparison. One loop is unimportant. Thousands become the work.

The canvas was already too small for the ambition. MoMA notes that Kusama spoke of extending the net over the entire world. In 1962 she began finding more literal ways to make that happen.

Infinity became a manufacturing problem

Accumulation No. 1 is a chair covered with hand-sewn, stuffed canvas forms. Others followed across furniture and larger surfaces. Kusama and assistants made thousands of these soft protrusions between 1962 and 1964.

The change from paint to sculpture did something important to repetition. On a canvas, a repeated mark costs a movement of the hand and a little paint. In the Accumulations, each repeated unit has to be cut, sewn, stuffed, attached, and often painted. The desire for “more” becomes physically expensive.

Kusama kept pushing anyway. She photographed Infinity Net paintings, cut the photographs apart, and recomposed them into new grids. She covered objects and rooms. By 1964, the Driving Image Show was already functioning as a total environment. The spectator could no longer encounter repetition as something safely contained in one framed object.

Then, in 1965, came Infinity Mirror Room—Phalli's Field.

The room contained a finite number of soft forms related to the Accumulation sculptures. Mirrors did the multiplying. Instead of sewing enough objects to fill an impossible space, Kusama could make a small field appear to continue in every direction.

This was more than an efficient production trick. Reflection changed the viewer's position. An Infinity Net can seem to continue beyond the canvas, but the viewer remains in front of it. In a mirror room, the viewer appears inside the repetition and becomes another repeated object.

The Hirshhorn describes the 1965 room as a breakthrough, and it was. But the breakthrough is easier to understand once the earlier work is visible. Mirrors did not give Kusama the idea of infinity. They gave her a way to stage an old idea at a scale that hand labor could never reach.

The earlier work makes the mirrors better

Kusama's mirror rooms eventually became among the most popular museum installations in contemporary art. Their photographic appeal is obvious. A camera does very well in a room where every light, dot, pumpkin, or body appears to reproduce itself without end.

That popularity can flatten the work if the rooms are encountered only as spectacular backgrounds. The paintings and Accumulations restore the difficulty to the effect. Before optical infinity, there was monotonous, stubborn accumulation: one painted loop after another, one stuffed form after another.

In some ways, those earlier works make repetition more legible because they preserve its cost. You can see that every unit had to be made. The mirrors erase that arithmetic. Twenty objects can look like twenty thousand.

The elegance of the mirror rooms lies partly in that leap. Kusama found a device that could make a finite artwork behave as though it had no edge. By 1965, she had spent years discovering exactly why she needed one.
