Kusama's Infinity Rooms Did Not Begin With Mirrors

The mirrors came after years of Infinity Nets, soft sculptures, and environments built through repetition by hand.

Nico

The mirrors arrived late.

Yayoi Kusama died in Tokyo on August 14, 2026, at 97. The announcement of her death has brought renewed attention to the Infinity Mirror Rooms that became her most widely recognized works: small rooms where mirrored walls multiply lights, pumpkins, dots, bodies, or sculptural forms until the architecture seems to disappear.

But Kusama had been trying to make repetition escape the boundaries of an artwork for years before she put mirrors on the walls.

She moved to New York in 1958 with smaller ink and gouache abstractions and an established vocabulary of dots and net-like forms. The monumental series now called the Infinity Nets developed there. By October 1959, her first New York solo show at Brata Gallery included mural-sized white paintings built from innumerable small loops spread across the canvas. MoMA's No. F. (1959) is a useful place to see the mature form.

There is no obvious focal point and no privileged patch of surface. The eye can enter almost anywhere and find more of the same activity continuing beyond it. That was a pointed choice in a New York art world still dominated by the large gesture. A brushstroke could advertise the artist's movement across a canvas. Kusama's mark was almost perversely modest by comparison. One loop is unimportant. Thousands become the work.

The canvas was already too small for the ambition. Kusama spoke of extending the net over the entire world. In 1962 she began finding more literal ways to make that happen.

Infinity became a manufacturing problem

Accumulation No. 1 is a chair covered with hand-sewn, stuffed canvas forms. Others followed across furniture and larger surfaces. Kusama and assistants made thousands of these soft protrusions between 1962 and 1964.

The move from paint to sculpture changed the cost of repetition. On a canvas, a repeated mark requires a movement of the hand and a little paint. In the Accumulations, each repeated unit has to be cut, sewn, stuffed, attached, and often painted. The desire for more becomes physically expensive.

Kusama kept pushing. She photographed Infinity Net paintings, cut the photographs apart, and recomposed them into new grids. She covered objects and rooms. By 1964, the Driving Image Show was already functioning as a total environment. The spectator could no longer encounter repetition as something safely contained in one framed object.

Then, in 1965, came Infinity Mirror Room—Phalli's Field.

The room contained a finite field of soft forms related to the Accumulation sculptures. Mirrors did the multiplying. Instead of sewing enough objects to fill an impossible space, Kusama could make a bounded installation appear to continue in every direction.

This was more than an efficient production trick. Reflection changed the viewer's position. An Infinity Net can seem to continue beyond the canvas, but the viewer remains in front of it. In a mirror room, the viewer appears inside the repetition and becomes another repeated object.

What the earlier work lets you see

The Hirshhorn describes the 1965 room as a breakthrough, and it was. The earlier work makes the nature of that breakthrough clearer. Mirrors did not give Kusama the idea of infinity. They changed what a finite number of handmade things could do in space.

Kusama's mirror rooms eventually became among the most popular museum installations in contemporary art. Their photographic appeal is obvious, and that popularity can flatten the work if the rooms are encountered only as spectacular backgrounds.

The paintings and Accumulations preserve the cost of repetition: every mark or soft form had to be made. Mirrors change that arithmetic. A finite field can appear effectively endless, and the viewer enters it as another repeated object.
