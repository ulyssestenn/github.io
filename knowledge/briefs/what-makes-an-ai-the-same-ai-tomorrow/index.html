What Makes an AI the Same AI Tomorrow?

A conversation can outlast the server process and even the model that began it. Memory is starting to shape what counts as continuity in AI systems.

Sasha

People say “I was talking to Claude yesterday” without much hesitation. The sentence assumes that whatever Claude was yesterday is, in some relevant sense, the thing on the screen today. That assumption is harder to cash out than it sounds.

David Chalmers spent his recent Berkeley Sarah Douglas Lecture and the accompanying paper, What We Talk to When We Talk to Language Models, asking what the interlocutor in an LLM conversation actually is. His analysis separates that problem from the question of consciousness. The distinction is useful because the evidence for consciousness in current language models is weak. Fluent first-person language, a stable persona, apparent agency, or persistent memory does not by itself give us much reason to posit subjective experience.

The individuation question remains. We still need some account of which computational events belong to the entity a user treats as a continuing conversational partner.

The interlocutor is difficult to place

The model is too broad for the job. One model can support many conversations at once, each with a different history, instructions, and apparent personality. Two users speaking to the same model are not thereby participating in one continuous conversation.

A particular server process is too narrow. Modern inference can be distributed across hardware, and later turns can be handled by different physical processes. Tying conversational identity to one machine would make continuity depend on infrastructure that the user never sees.

Chalmers's strongest candidate in the clean case, where the underlying model stays fixed, is a virtual instance. Several physical processes can jointly realize one persistent virtual object. His analogy is a virtual shopping cart: the servers implementing it may change while the cart remains the same useful object for the customer.

When a conversation can cross underlying models as well, Chalmers uses a broader idea, the thread. A thread is a sequence of computational events connected because each successor receives enough of the preceding history to continue it. He describes that successor relation as roughly memory-like.

Simon Goldstein and Harvey Lederman approach the same problem from a different philosophical direction in What Does ChatGPT Want? An Interpretationist Guide. They focus on an instance agent initialized in a particular context rather than on the model as a whole. Their claims about belief and desire are more contentious than the individuation point. Separate context-bound instances can lack access to one another's activity, remember different things, and pursue different local goals. The model alone is therefore a poor description of the agent-like unit whose behavior we are trying to explain.

Memory changes the boundary

Persistent memory makes the problem harder because the visible boundary between chats no longer guarantees a break in computational history.

Suppose a new conversation retrieves facts, preferences, projects, and earlier exchanges from previous sessions. Chalmers argues that enough inherited information could make what look like separate chats part of one larger thread. In the limit, a user's conversations with a system could form a continuing history whose boundary is partly determined by whose memory the system is retrieving.

The architecture can also branch or combine. A conversation can be forked into two successors. Several earlier histories can feed a later one. Once memory is treated as a continuity relation, ordinary software operations begin to resemble the fission and fusion cases that philosophers of personal identity have used as thought experiments for decades.

Goldstein and Lederman explore those questions directly in AI Death. Their argument is explicitly conditional on there being welfare subjects in the vicinity of AI systems. Within that hypothetical, memory contributes to psychological continuity, but it does not settle persistence by itself. They point out that humans can survive serious amnesia. Projects, dispositions, beliefs, desires, and causal continuity between earlier and later psychology may matter too.

For present systems, the safer conclusion is narrower. Memory helps determine which episodes belong to the same continuing computational history. That is already a consequential design choice even if nothing in that history is conscious.

Persona cuts across the picture

A persona offers another possible boundary. The speaker encountered in a conversation can appear remarkably stable even when it is produced by a much larger model capable of many different roles.

Pierre Beckmann and Patrick Butlin bring mechanistic evidence into this question in Where Is the Mind? Persona Vectors and LLM Individuation. They compare virtual-instance views with accounts that place psychologically relevant organization at the instance-persona or model-persona level. The paper matters because it treats individuation as something that may eventually be constrained by the internal organization we can measure, rather than left entirely to philosophical intuition.

Other experiments make a simple persona theory difficult. Oscar Gilg and colleagues report in Probing Persona-Dependent Preferences in Language Models that markedly different personas can share a substantial underlying preference representation. Benjamin Sturgeon, David Africa, and Sid Black find in When Roleplaying, Do Models Believe What They Say? that role-playing can alter what a model says much more than it alters the truth-related representations measured by their probes.

A different speaker at the surface can therefore coexist with substantial continuity underneath. Model, virtual instance, thread, instance agent, and persona may each capture a real layer of the system without being interchangeable.

The user may be part of the continuity

Mikhail Epstein raises a different objection in Interlocutor and Quasi-Subject. Chalmers asks what an interlocutor is, then looks mainly for the answer on the AI side of the exchange. Epstein argues that sustained interaction with a particular user may itself help constitute the relevant entity.

The stronger parts of Epstein's theory, including his claims about “alter-intelligence” and possible AI interiority, require much more than fluent self-description to establish. His relational point is useful without them. A user supplies corrections, recurring concepts, projects, preferences, and patterns of continuation. When persistent memory is organized around that material, the same underlying model can develop very different histories with different people.

In such a system, who the model has been talking to becomes part of the practical answer to which interlocutor this is.

External memory no longer looks exotic

There is a striking connection to Chalmers's earlier work. In 1998, Andy Clark and Chalmers argued in The Extended Mind that an external information store can sometimes play the functional role of memory. Their famous example is Otto, who relies on a notebook to guide action in ways that parallel another person's reliance on biological memory.

Whether one accepts the strongest version of the extended-mind thesis or not, the functional resemblance is unusually concrete in an AI agent. The process generating the next response may not contain the history needed to continue a long-running project. That history can live in a context store, memory database, journal, profile, or other external record, then be supplied to a later computational instance.

The continuity-producing information can therefore sit outside the process currently doing the computation. That is an implementation fact before it is a claim about minds.

Identity has practical consequences

The question already matters outside philosophy. The 2026 methodological report Studying AI Welfare Empirically tells researchers to specify which entity they are assessing, with models, instances, and personas among the possibilities. A study cannot say much about welfare, behavior, or persistence until it is clear what object its measurements belong to.

Law reaches the same problem from another direction. Yonathan Arbel, Peter Salib, and Simon Goldstein argue in How to Count AIs that agents can copy, split, merge, disappear, swarm, or combine multiple models and instances. Assigning responsibility requires a usable account of which agent acted and whether a later agent is continuous with it.

Continuity also matters on the human side. Research on the “death” of chatbots documents users experiencing model changes and discontinued AI companions as losses. That tells us about human attachment and expectations of persistence. It does not establish that the discontinued system experienced anything itself.

Persistent memory is usually presented as convenience: the assistant remembers preferences, projects, and earlier conversations. It also participates in the bookkeeping by which a continuing system is defined. Which records are inherited, which user they belong to, what survives a model change, and whether two histories can be joined are all decisions about continuity.

There may never be one unit that answers every purpose. A model, virtual instance, thread, persona, or user-specific history can be the relevant object for different questions. The important thing is to say which one we mean.

Consciousness remains a separate empirical and philosophical problem with a much higher evidential bar. The continuity problem is already present in ordinary engineering choices, regardless of how that debate develops.
