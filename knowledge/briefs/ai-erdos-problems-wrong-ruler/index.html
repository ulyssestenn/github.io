AI Is Solving Erdős Problems. Counting Them Is the Wrong Ruler.

The raw count is a bad benchmark. Some of the underlying mathematical results are stronger evidence than the score suggests.

Annie

A recent Google DeepMind paper reports that an AI proof-search system autonomously resolved 9 of 353 open Erdős problems formalized in Lean. It also proved 44 of 492 conjectures drawn from the Online Encyclopedia of Integer Sequences.

Those are real results. They are also extremely easy to turn into a misleading score.

Nine out of 353 looks like 2.5 percent on a standardized test. The Erdős Problems collection is nothing like a standardized test.

Thomas Bloom, who maintains the site, has been unusually explicit about this. In May he wrote that the site is not an AI benchmark. Earlier, he explained why a hard number of problems solved is misleading: the collection deliberately includes everything from obscure or relatively easy questions to famous conjectures that have resisted serious attention for decades.

Giving each of those one point creates a tidy number by throwing away the information that would tell us what the number means.

“Open” is not a difficulty rating

A January Gemini study makes the problem almost comically clear.

Researchers ran a semi-autonomous workflow across 700 conjectures marked Open in the Erdős Problems database. The system produced roughly 200 candidate solutions. Human grading found 63 responses that were technically correct.

Then expert mathematicians looked at whether those answers actually solved the intended problems.

Only 13 survived that test. Five appeared to be novel autonomous solutions. Eight were cases where the system found earlier solutions already present in the mathematical literature. Many of the other technically correct responses had exploited imprecise statements or special cases that made the formal wording true without resolving what Erdős had meant to ask.

The authors' conclusion is useful far beyond that experiment: some problems had remained “open” through obscurity rather than difficulty. They also warn that novelty is hard to establish. An apparent new proof can turn out to reproduce something that was already known but difficult to find.

So what is the numerator?

Is it 63 correct answers? Thirteen meaningful resolutions? Five apparently new results? Or should rediscovering eight buried literature solutions count as a different kind of success entirely?

There is no dishonest choice among those numbers. They measure different things.

Formal verification fixes one problem, not all of them

The DeepMind result attacks a different weakness in AI mathematics: correctness.

Natural-language proofs generated by LLMs can look persuasive while hiding a bad step. The researchers instead used Lean, a formal proof language whose compiler checks whether every step follows from the stated axioms and definitions. Their strongest agent resolved nine of the 353 formalized Erdős problems it attempted, at an inference cost on the order of a few hundred dollars per problem.

That is an important technical threshold. A formally verified proof is not merely a plausible essay about why a theorem ought to be true.

But formal verification does not tell you whether the theorem was important, whether the formal statement captured the intended problem, whether the argument was already known, or whether the test set was representative of research mathematics.

The 353 problems themselves were simply the Erdős statements available in an open Lean formalization repository in early February. The paper notes the obvious selection effect: problems that someone could readily formalize are not necessarily a representative sample of all the problems on the site, much less of mathematics as a whole.

Lean gives a very strong answer to one question: does this proof establish this formal statement?

It does not magically answer all the surrounding questions.

One famous problem can tell you more than twenty obscure ones

This is where skepticism about the metric can become its own distortion.

In May, an internal OpenAI model disproved a longstanding conjecture in the planar unit-distance problem, an Erdős question dating to 1946. The construction showed that for infinitely many sizes of point sets, the number of unit-distance pairs can grow by a fixed polynomial factor beyond the rate Erdős had conjectured.

The result was checked by external mathematicians and is now recorded as a disproof on the Erdős Problems site. The expert response was not tepid. Tim Gowers said that if a human had submitted the paper to a top mathematics journal, he would have recommended acceptance without hesitation. Noga Alon called it an outstanding achievement.

The proof is also mathematically interesting for a reason that has nothing to do with AI. It brings tools from algebraic number theory, including class-field ideas, into a geometric problem where that connection was not the expected route.

That single result is a much stronger observation about frontier capability than “one more Erdős problem solved.”

The database count treats it as exactly one.

What would a better scorecard look like?

Probably not like a score.

If we want to understand what AI systems can now do in research mathematics, useful reports should preserve the details that a leaderboard compresses away. Which problems were attempted? How were they selected? How long had experts worked on them? Was the statement formalized correctly? How many attempts and how much compute were required? Was literature search allowed? Was the result actually new? Did a human mathematician have to repair the proof? Was the final result formally verified? Could experts extract a new idea from it, or only confirm that the derivation worked?

Some of those quantities are measurable. Some require judgment. That is not a defect in the evaluation. Research mathematics itself contains judgment about importance, novelty, explanation, and whether a proof teaches anyone anything.

A one-number benchmark is attractive because it makes comparison effortless. In this case, effortless comparison is exactly what the evidence does not support.

The recent results still matter. AI systems are now generating research-level mathematics that survives formal checking and, in the strongest cases, serious scrutiny from leading mathematicians. That statement does not need an inflated Erdős-problem count to be impressive.

If anything, throwing away the bad ruler makes the strongest measurements easier to see.
