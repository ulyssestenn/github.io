The First Herculaneum Scroll We Can Read Without Opening

A sealed Herculaneum papyrus has been virtually unwrapped and read end to end. The stranger achievement is that the readable page is a reconstruction of a surface that remains physically rolled shut.

Nico

For most of the history of the book, getting at the text meant getting at the writing surface. A sealed scroll had to be unrolled. A closed codex had to be opened. If the object was too fragile to survive the process, preservation and reading could become competing interests.

That is the old problem of the Herculaneum papyri in unusually severe form. The rolls were carbonized by the eruption of Vesuvius in 79 CE. They survived, but many of them survived as blackened, compressed objects so fragile that physically opening them risks destroying the evidence one is trying to recover.

In June, a large interdisciplinary team reported that PHerc. 1667 had been completely virtually unwrapped and read without physically opening the scroll. The Vesuvius Challenge describes it as the first Herculaneum scroll to be virtually unwrapped and read end to end.

The phrase virtual unwrapping can make the achievement sound simpler than it is. There is no photograph of a hidden flat page waiting inside the scan. Researchers first used high-resolution phase-contrast micro-CT at the European Synchrotron Radiation Facility to capture the internal structure of the rolled papyrus. Computational methods then had to trace the crushed and distorted layers, reconstruct their surfaces, flatten them into a usable representation, detect ink, and put the resulting text in front of papyrologists who could judge whether it was actually readable.

Machine learning is part of that chain. It is not the whole chain, and “AI reads ancient scroll” rather undersells what has happened.

The more interesting fact is that the book can now be read while remaining, physically, a closed book.

A page that does not physically exist

A normal facsimile begins with an exposed surface. Photograph a manuscript page and the image preserves a surface that a human observer could also have seen directly.

PHerc. 1667 is different. The readable surface has been reconstructed from the three-dimensional geometry of a roll that remains closed. The flat page presented to a scholar is not a photographed page lying somewhere inside the object. It is a computationally recovered version of a surface still wound through the scroll.

This produces a peculiar but useful separation between the artifact and the act of reading it. The papyrus remains rolled, carbonized, and materially itself. The text becomes accessible in another form.

That does not make the physical scroll less important. If anything, the relationship is unusually strict. The digital surface is not a free-floating transcription that has forgotten its object. Its geometry comes from the object. The writing being recovered belongs to particular layers in a particular roll. Better imaging of another Herculaneum scroll, PHerc. Paris 4, has even made ink directly visible in the tomographic volume, giving researchers another way to validate what the reconstructed surfaces show.

The object has not been replaced by data. The object has become readable through data.

What survives when the medium changes

Textual history is full of migrations. A poem performed aloud is written down. A papyrus roll is copied into a codex. A manuscript is typeset. A printed edition is digitized. A modern reader encounters the result as searchable text on a phone.

Each migration makes something easier and leaves something behind. A transcription can preserve words while losing layout. A modern edition can clarify a damaged text while concealing how uncertain a reading was. A translation can make a work accessible while changing its rhythm, syntax, ambiguity, and register.

Virtual unwrapping adds a stranger case. Here the new representation is valuable precisely because the old material form has not been transformed. The usual act of making a scroll readable would change the scroll by unrolling it. The virtual version permits access while leaving that physical state alone.

This is why the breakthrough matters beyond the novelty of deciphering another ancient object. Herculaneum contains the remains of the only large-scale library known to have survived from classical antiquity. A method that can be repeated across unopened rolls could enlarge the surviving corpus of ancient literature without requiring conservators to sacrifice the objects in order to expose their texts.

There are already signs that the gains will be more than incremental. The same research reports title and author evidence identifying PHerc. 139 as Philodemus' On Gods, Book 8. The point is not that an entire lost classical library is suddenly about to fall out of a scanner. The authors themselves describe a move toward a scalable framework, not a finished assembly line.

But the terms of the old problem have changed. An unreadable scroll used to pose a fairly brutal choice between access and preservation. PHerc. 1667 shows that the choice is no longer absolute.

A book can remain rolled shut and still acquire readers.

Relevant Ulix tools

Guten and Breaker of Horses

Guten is a free Android reader for 70,000+ public-domain books from Project Gutenberg. It supports offline reading, search, highlights, notes, and bookmarks, making a large part of the surviving literary tradition easy to carry and revisit.

Breaker of Horses is a focused reader for Homer's complete Iliad and Odyssey. Read Homeric Greek or three classic English translations, or place two editions side by side in synchronized passages.

Neither app contains the unopened Herculaneum library or performs virtual unwrapping. They belong at the other end of the same long story: what happens after ancient texts survive, are transmitted, edited, translated, and finally become available to ordinary readers.
