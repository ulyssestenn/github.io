How to Build a Reading Collection Around a Question Instead of a Genre

History, philosophy, and novels are perfectly sensible ways to sort books. They are not always very good ways to build a reading project.

Nico

History, philosophy, and novels are perfectly sensible ways to sort books. They are not always very good ways to build a reading project.

A genre tells you what kind of book something is. A question tells you why several books belong together for you right now.

That distinction matters in a public-domain library because the available material crosses centuries, forms, languages, and disciplines. A question can put a speech beside a novel, a memoir beside a treatise, or two writers who would never share a bookstore shelf into the same temporary conversation.

Guten's custom Collections are useful for this because the collection does not have to become a permanent classification system. It can simply hold the books needed for one line of inquiry.

Give the collection a sentence, not a category

Compare these two collection names:

Victorian Literature

and

What did Victorians think progress was for?

The first can expand indefinitely. The second has a reason to admit some books and exclude others.

A good question-based collection usually has enough specificity that you can look at a candidate book and decide whether it contributes something.

Other examples:

  • What did Roman writers think a good citizen owed the state?
  • How did nineteenth-century writers describe industrial work?
  • What changes when a ghost story is also a moral argument?
  • How did abolitionists argue with readers who already knew slavery was cruel?
  • What did early science fiction imagine technology would do to ordinary life?
  • How do different writers describe the tension between duty and private desire?

These are not search queries carved in stone. They are provisional reading structures.

Mix forms when the question benefits from it

Genre shelves encourage like to sit beside like. A question may be better served by contrast.

Suppose the question is What did Americans think republican virtue required? A collection might include political speeches, essays, historical writing, letters, and fiction. The differences among those forms become part of the inquiry. A sermon and a constitutional argument may use some of the same moral vocabulary for very different purposes.

Likewise, a collection on What makes revenge feel like justice? does not need to contain only tragedies. An epic, a novel, a philosophical essay, and a historical account may illuminate different parts of the same problem.

The point is not to create a syllabus that looks impressively interdisciplinary. It is to allow the question to determine the shelf.

Keep the collection small enough to change your mind

A question-based collection can become another unread-book warehouse if every remotely relevant title goes into it.

Start with perhaps three to six books. Enough for comparison, not enough to make beginning feel absurd.

As you read, the question may narrow or change. That is useful information.

How did Victorians think about progress? may become Why did technological progress so often get described in moral language? Several books that looked central at the beginning may now be peripheral. Remove them. Add something better.

A Collection is more useful when it reflects the current state of the inquiry than when it preserves every earlier intention.

Use one anchor book and then create friction around it

A particularly good way to start is with one book you already want to read.

Suppose the anchor is Walden. Instead of creating a collection called American Classics, ask what question the book raises that you would like another writer to answer differently.

Perhaps: What is enough?

Now Walden might sit beside older Stoic writing, Ruskin, political economy, or another text concerned with labor, possession, and the good life. The collection is not claiming those books agree. Agreement is often less interesting than useful disagreement.

Or begin with Frankenstein and ask What obligations does a maker have toward what he creates? The resulting collection can move into theology, political thought, science, or other fiction without pretending they form one genre.

Search Project Gutenberg by subject, then ignore the subject boundaries

Guten can browse and search the Project Gutenberg catalog by title, author, language, or subject. Subject terms are useful for discovery, but they do not have to determine the final collection.

Search broadly enough to find candidates, then inspect the book descriptions and decide whether the text actually bears on your question.

This is an important difference between discovery metadata and a reading project. The catalog's subject vocabulary has to describe what a book is about for many users. Your Collection only has to describe why you want these particular books in relation to one another.

Write the question somewhere outside the collection name when it gets complicated

Some reading questions do not fit gracefully into a short Collection title.

Use a compact name such as Roman virtue or Making and obligation, then keep a note with the fuller question and any subquestions that emerge.

For example:

Collection: Roman virtue

Working note:

  • What does public virtue demand that private morality does not?
  • How do Cicero and later Christian writers use the same Roman vocabulary differently?
  • Which virtues belong to office, and which belong to the person?

Now the collection remains easy to scan while the reading project has enough precision to guide attention.

Let the collection end

Genre categories are usually permanent. A question does not have to be.

When the project has done its work, export or preserve the notes you care about, then delete or repurpose the Collection if it no longer helps. The books remain in the library. The temporary shelf has served its purpose.

This is one of the advantages of organizing around inquiry rather than identity. You do not need to become a person who reads political philosophy in order to spend six weeks reading one political question seriously.

A collection can simply be the shape a curiosity takes while it is alive.
