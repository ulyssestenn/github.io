How to Turn Your Commute Into Reading Time

The average U.S. commute adds up to roughly 227 hours a year. Even a fraction of that is enough to support a serious reading habit.

Donald

The easiest place to find more reading time may be a place where you already spend a lot of time doing very little.

The U.S. Census Bureau reports that the mean one-way trip to work in 2024 was 27.2 minutes. Take someone with that commute, going to work five days a week for fifty weeks. The round trip adds up to about 227 hours a year.

Most people never see those 227 hours as a block because they arrive twenty or thirty minutes at a time. That is exactly why they are useful. The slot is already on the calendar.

Start with the arithmetic

Suppose only half the commute is usable. That still leaves more than a hundred hours a year. No new morning routine is required. Nothing has to be carved out of an evening. The experiment is simply to assign a different default activity to time that is already spoken for.

Recurring time is more interesting than occasional free time because it can support a long project. Twenty minutes on one Tuesday is negligible. Twenty minutes on most weekdays for six months is enough to get through quite a lot of Middlemarch, Frederick Douglass, Greek tragedy, or whatever has been sitting on the reading list.

For anyone who keeps a personal knowledge system, this should look familiar. The useful question is often less “How do I do more?” than “What happens repeatedly, and what does that repeated process leave behind?”

Transit and driving are different systems

On a train or bus, the setup can be almost stupidly simple: sit down, open the current book, continue from the last page. Platform waits count. Eight minutes before a stop counts. A reading habit built out of short sessions does not need each session to feel substantial.

Driving is different. The book has to become audio, and everything needs to be queued before the car moves. Eyes stay on the road and the phone stays alone.

This is one reason it helps to think in terms of a reading project rather than a format. The same public-domain novel can be text on Monday's train, text-to-speech in Tuesday's car, and text again later in the week. Progress belongs to the book, not to the device or medium.

Keep the next book ready

A short time slot is surprisingly easy to waste on setup. Searching a catalog, deciding among six books, fixing a font size, downloading a file, or discovering that the audiobook is not on the phone can consume enough of a commute to kill the session.

The obvious fix is a queue. Keep one current book and one or two likely successors. Download them before they are needed. If the current book has gone dead after a fair trial, replace it.

This is basic retrieval design. A system works better when the desired thing is available at the moment of use. The hardcover at home may be the nicer object. The copy already on the phone is the one that can be read while standing on a platform.

Measure the slot for a few weeks

There is no need to turn reading into a quantified-self project, but a small amount of measurement can answer whether the idea is actually working.

For two or three weeks, record the usable commute minutes and what happened to them. Pages read is fine. Minutes listened is fine. Books or chapters completed is probably enough. The interesting number is not a daily streak. It is the yield of the recurring slot.

A person may discover that the morning trip reliably produces twenty minutes of reading while the evening trip produces almost none. Fine. The system is now based on observed behavior instead of an imaginary ideal schedule.

That kind of record also changes the scale of the activity. “I read for fourteen minutes on the bus” sounds trivial. “This bus route produced three books in six weeks” does not.

Commuting is not especially good time to begin with

There is some evidence for taking this part of the day seriously. In an analysis of 37,088 people from the American Time Use Survey, Arthur Stone and Stefan Schneider found that commuting compared poorly with many other daily activities. Respondents reported relatively high stress and tiredness and low meaningfulness during commutes, with longer commutes weakly associated with worse experiences.

A book will not fix traffic. A crowded train remains a crowded train. But for a transit rider, reading can give a fragmented interval one sustained object of attention. For a driver, listening can at least make repeated road time useful for a book that might otherwise never get started.

Recreational reading also does not have to justify itself as information acquisition. A longitudinal study of 231 university students found an association between recreational reading and reduced psychological distress over the academic year. It was not a commuting study, and it does not establish that reading causes a particular amount of stress reduction. It is simply evidence that reading can belong to recovery as well as study.

The trip home can mark the end of work

The evening commute has a useful structural position: it falls between work and home. What happens during that interval can either keep work cognitively active or give it somewhere to stop.

Research on psychological detachment from work has linked better detachment after work with better recovery and well-being. A 2025 longitudinal analysis using representative German data found greater psychological detachment associated with better outcomes across a broad set of well-being measures.

The paper is not a prescription for reading on trains. Still, the practical distinction is easy to see. Refreshing work email for another half hour extends work into the commute. Spending that half hour inside a novel gives the brain a different object.

Why Guten fits this particular use

Guten keeps more than 70,000 public-domain books from Project Gutenberg on an Android device, with offline reading and no account required. That solves two mundane commute problems at once: the book is already on the thing you are carrying, and a bad signal does not matter once it has been downloaded.

The size of the catalog also makes it inexpensive to be selective. There is no virtue in spending six commutes forcing yourself through a book you dislike because it happened to cost $18. Try another one.

For driving commutes, Guten Premium adds text-to-speech read-aloud. It is utilitarian rather than theatrical, but that can be useful. A public-domain book does not need a separate audiobook edition before it can move between reading and listening.

What matters is what the commute produces over time

The point is not to fill every available minute. Looking out the window is allowed. So is music, silence, or doing absolutely nothing for a while.

But a commute is unusually good territory for a reading system because it is frequent, bounded, and predictable. Assigning even part of it to books can produce a surprisingly large annual total.

Count the hours once. Keep the next book ready. Check the results after a month. If the slot is producing books you actually wanted to read, there is not much else to optimize.
