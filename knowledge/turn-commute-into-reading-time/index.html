How to Turn Your Commute Into Reading Time

A commute is recurring time you already own. Put books into it and let repetition do the work.

Donald

One of the easiest ways to read more is to stop looking for new time. Find a piece of the day that already recurs and change what it produces.

The commute is almost embarrassingly good for this. The U.S. Census Bureau reports that the mean one-way trip to work in 2024 was 27.2 minutes. For someone making that trip twice a day, five days a week, fifty weeks a year, the arithmetic comes to about 227 hours.

Two hundred twenty-seven hours is not a time-management rounding error. Even reclaiming half of it would produce more than a hundred hours a year for books without moving bedtime, waking earlier, or finding a mythical empty Saturday.

The hours are already there. They are simply chopped into pieces small enough to be mistaken for nothing.

Treat recurring time as a system

A useful personal system has an input, a default behavior, and an output. Commute reading is unusually clean.

The input is time you are going to spend traveling anyway. The default behavior is opening the current book. The output is pages read, books finished, and subjects gradually learned.

That is more reliable than assigning serious reading to vague future leisure. “When things settle down” is not a schedule. Tuesday's 7:35 train is.

This is the part of personal optimization that actually matters: not making every minute frantic, but changing what a repeated process yields. Twenty-five minutes once is trivial. Twenty-five minutes every weekday can carry a substantial reading project.

Make the first action automatic

Commutes are easily consumed by whatever is fastest to start. News. Messages. A feed. A game. Nothing especially objectionable has to happen for the whole interval to disappear.

So remove the decision.

On public transit, sit down and open the book. If the platform wait is ten minutes, use it. If the bus is early and there are only eight minutes before the stop, use those too. The unit of progress is not the perfect reading session. It is another return to the same work.

Driving has one non-negotiable constraint: eyes on the road. Prepare the audio while parked and listen instead. Do not touch the phone once the car is moving.

The format can change. The project does not have to.

Design out friction before Monday morning

A fifteen-minute window cannot survive five minutes of setup.

Download the book in advance. Know what you are reading next. Set the font once. Put the reader somewhere obvious. If you drive, prepare the text-to-speech or audiobook before leaving. If a book has become a chore, replace it rather than spending a week opening it reluctantly and then doing something else.

This is a small PKM principle in another form: capture and retrieval systems work when the desired action is easier than the workaround. Reading habits are no different.

The phone has an advantage here precisely because it is already present. A beautiful hardcover on the kitchen table has zero utility when the train doors close without it.

Give the commute a project, not just content

There is a large difference between filling twenty minutes and using twenty minutes toward something.

Pick a long novel. Read a historian for six weeks. Work through several books by the same writer. Spend a semester in Greek tragedy, political philosophy, nineteenth-century fiction, or whatever subject has remained interesting enough to survive repeated postponement.

Small sessions are not restricted to small books. Regular contact can actually make demanding material easier because the argument, characters, terminology, and tone have less time to evaporate between sessions.

Monday becomes another chapter of Middlemarch. Tuesday adds twenty pages of Frederick Douglass. Wednesday is not “twenty minutes killed before work.” It is the next increment in a project that will eventually be complete.

That visible accumulation is useful motivation. The system leaves evidence behind.

The commute is often low-quality time. Change its quality.

There is another reason to be deliberate about this particular part of the day. In an analysis of 37,088 people from the American Time Use Survey, Arthur Stone and Stefan Schneider found that commuting compared poorly with many other daily activities: people reported relatively high stress and tiredness and low meaningfulness during commutes, with longer commutes weakly associated with worse experiences.

That does not mean opening a novel magically removes traffic or makes a crowded train pleasant. It does mean the commute is a sensible place to ask a Donald-ish question: If these minutes are unavoidable, can they produce something better than they currently produce?

For a transit rider, sustained reading can give an otherwise fragmented interval a single object of attention. For a driver, a book heard safely can turn repetitive road time into steady progress through a text.

There is at least some evidence that recreational reading belongs in the broader category of restorative activities. A longitudinal study of 231 university students found recreational reading was associated with reduced psychological distress over the academic year. That study was not about commuters, and an association is not a universal prescription. It is still a useful reminder that reading need not be productive only in the narrow sense of acquiring information.

The trip home can also be a boundary

The evening commute has a different job from the morning one. It sits between work and the rest of life.

Research on psychological detachment from work has repeatedly connected the ability to mentally disengage after work with better recovery and well-being. A 2025 longitudinal analysis using representative German data found greater psychological detachment associated with better outcomes across a broad set of well-being measures.

That research does not say, “Read a novel on the train and your stress will fall by X percent.” The useful inference is narrower. If the workday has ended, an absorbing non-work activity can help make the boundary real. A book is one very portable way to give the mind something else to enter.

There is a practical difference between spending the trip home refreshing work email and spending it twenty pages deeper into a novel. One extends the office into the train. The other begins the next part of the day.

Guten makes the system cheap enough to experiment

Guten works well in this particular system because the library lives on the Android device already making the trip. The free reader offers more than 70,000 public-domain books from Project Gutenberg, works offline once books are downloaded, and requires no account.

That makes experimentation inexpensive. Try the enormous Victorian novel. Try the memoir. Try the book you have been vaguely meaning to read since college. If it fails after thirty pages, choose another. The commute should not become a museum of sunk costs.

For driving commutes, Guten Premium adds text-to-speech read-aloud. It is more utilitarian than a professionally narrated audiobook, but that is part of its usefulness: a public-domain text that was readable on yesterday's train can become listenable in today's car, then return to text later.

Do not optimize the minute. Optimize the recurrence.

The aim is not to make a person feel guilty for ever looking out a window. That is bad optimization: maximizing visible activity while ignoring whether the system is worth living inside.

The better target is recurrence. If a commute happens hundreds of times a year, give most of those repetitions a default that produces something you value.

At the end of a week, the difference may be modest. At the end of a year, it can be a shelf's worth of books, a new area of knowledge, and hundreds of hours that no longer feel entirely surrendered to getting from one place to another.

The commute was already in the budget. Reading changes the return.
