How to Turn Your Commute Into Reading Time

A commute is already on the calendar. The trick is not finding more time but deciding what that repeated half hour is going to contain.

Donald

A commute is one of the easiest places to find reading time because the time already exists. A half hour each way, five days a week, is five hours. That is enough time for a real reading life to accumulate without adding a single appointment to the calendar.

The useful question is not “How can I become the sort of person who reads for two serene hours every night?” It is much smaller: what can happen during the forty minutes already spent on the train, the bus, or behind the wheel?

Let the mode follow the commute

On a train or bus, the obvious answer is visual reading. A phone is already easy to hold, easy to stop when the station arrives, and less cumbersome than carrying several physical books.

In a car, reading is not an option. Listen instead. Set up the book before the vehicle is moving, then leave the phone alone and use audio for the drive.

That distinction sounds obvious, but it makes the habit easier because there is no single ideal format to protect. The book can fit the situation rather than the situation having to fit the book.

Public-domain reading is unusually good for experimentation

Buying a book creates a tiny psychological obligation. Even a disappointing purchase can produce the feeling that it ought to be finished because money has already been spent on it.

A large public-domain library removes much of that pressure. Try Middlemarch. Try Frederick Douglass. Try a detective story, a translation of an ancient text, a Victorian travel book, a strange nineteenth-century memoir, or a novel that has been sitting on a mental “someday” list for fifteen years.

If twenty pages reveal that it is not the right book for this commute, choose another one. Nothing has been wasted except twenty pages of honest experimentation.

That makes Guten useful as a low-cost complement to commercial reading services. Its center of gravity is more than 70,000 public-domain books from Project Gutenberg, and downloaded books can be read offline.

Kindle and Audible solve a different part of the problem

Kindle is an obvious choice when the book is contemporary, when a particular commercial edition matters, or when an existing Amazon library is already established. Audible is excellent when professional narration is part of the appeal.

There is no reason to pretend those options disappear just because Guten exists.

Guten is attractive for a different reason. The public-domain catalog makes it easy to try classics and older books freely and keep them on an Android device without creating an account. The free reader covers ordinary reading. Guten Premium adds text-to-speech read-aloud for people who want the same book available in listening situations too.

Professional audiobooks and text-to-speech are not the same experience. A great narrator can be part of the art of an audiobook. Text-to-speech is more utilitarian. Its advantage is that a readable public-domain text can also become listenable when the commute changes from train to car.

Use the same book in different parts of the day

A book does not have to belong exclusively to “reading time.” It can be the thing read for twenty minutes on the morning train, heard during an afternoon drive, and opened again before bed.

Guten’s read-aloud feature and visual reader live with the same book, although they should not be thought of as an audiobook service with perfect cross-mode position synchronization. The useful part is simpler: the text does not vanish from the day when looking at a screen stops being practical.

Dense or beautiful passages may still deserve the page. A plot-heavy chapter may work perfectly well aloud. The reader can decide moment by moment which mode serves the book better.

Build a commute shelf, not an aspirational library

The easiest mistake is to download twenty-five improving books and create another backlog.

A smaller shelf works better. Keep one book that is easy to enter for ten minutes, one longer book worth living with for a month, and perhaps one wildcard that would never have been purchased on speculation. When one stalls, move to another without ceremony.

The point is not to prove seriousness. It is to keep a good book available at the exact moment boredom usually wins.

Make starting require almost nothing

Before the week begins, download the books. Set the font size and theme. Put the reader somewhere easy to reach on the phone. If the commute is by car, choose the book and start the listening setup while parked.

Then remove decisions from the commute itself.

The train arrives: open the book. The bus is late: open the book. The car starts: use audio. Ten minutes appear between connections: the current book is already there.

This is the same reason a physical paperback left in a bag gets read more often than a worthy book left on a shelf at home. Availability changes behavior.

Do not turn the commute into a productivity contest

There is a slightly grim version of self-improvement in which every unoccupied minute becomes raw material to be optimized. A commute does not need to become that.

Sometimes silence is better. Sometimes music is better. Sometimes looking out the window is exactly what a tired mind needs.

The goal is not to eliminate unproductive time. It is to notice that many commutes are already being spent in ways that do not feel especially restorative or memorable. If reading would be better, the time is available.

Five hours a week does not sound dramatic while it is happening in half-hour pieces. Over a semester, a work year, or a long season of commuting, it becomes a great many pages.

A commute is time that keeps coming back

That repetition is the useful part. One isolated twenty-minute interval does not look important. The same interval returning every weekday can carry a novel, then another, then another.

No elaborate reading plan is required. Keep something worth reading or hearing within reach, use the interval when it suits the day, and let repetition do the arithmetic.

A commute is time that keeps coming back. Put books in it.
