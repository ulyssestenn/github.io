How to Turn Your Commute Into Reading Time

A commute is recurring time you already own. Put books into it and let repetition do the work.

Donald

One of the easiest ways to read more is to stop looking for new time and reclaim time that is already committed. A commute is unusually good for this. It happens again tomorrow, and the day after that, whether anything useful is done with it or not.

A thirty-minute trip each way, five days a week, adds up to five hours. Over a sixteen-week semester, that is eighty hours. Over a full year of weekly commuting, it is roughly 260 hours. Seen one ride at a time, the intervals look disposable. Seen together, they are a substantial block of life.

Reading can make that block productive in the most literal way: books get finished, subjects become familiar, difficult works stop being perpetually postponed, and knowledge accumulates without asking for another evening on the calendar.

Decide that the commute has a job

Commute time tends to be claimed by whatever is easiest to start. News, messages, social feeds, games, radio, or simply nothing in particular can fill the interval before there has been any conscious decision about it.

A reading habit works better when the decision has already been made. The train is not a place where you decide whether to read. It is where you continue the book. The car is not where you browse for something to hear. It is where you resume listening.

This is a small distinction, but it removes the daily negotiation. The commute acquires a default use.

Use the format the commute permits

On a train, subway, ferry, or bus, read normally. Ten or twenty minutes is enough to move through a chapter, and a phone is already the one object almost everyone has within reach.

If you drive, the rule is simpler: do not read. Use audio instead, set it up before the vehicle moves, and leave the phone alone while driving.

The useful habit is not attachment to a format. It is continuity with the book. Visual reading when your eyes are free and listening when they are not can turn two different kinds of commute into the same long-running reading project.

Keep the current book ready before you leave

The easiest way to waste a short interval is to spend the first third of it deciding what to do.

Have the book downloaded. Know what you are reading next. Set the font size in advance. If you will be listening, have the book open before you start driving. A commute is too short to support much setup, but it is long enough to support a surprising amount of reading once setup disappears.

This is why convenience matters more than ambition. A serious book that is already open gets read. A perfectly planned reading list buried behind six decisions does not.

Choose books that reward continuity

Commute reading does not have to mean easy reading. Long novels, history, biography, philosophy, essays, and classics all benefit from regular contact. In fact, a difficult book can become less difficult when it is encountered every weekday instead of being reopened after ten days away.

The useful criterion is not whether a book is lightweight. It is whether you can re-enter it without spending half the ride reconstructing where you were.

Some readers will want one long book for the whole month. Others may pair a demanding book with something easier for tired days. Either approach works. What matters is that the commute keeps the reading project moving.

Let small sessions accumulate instead of waiting for ideal ones

People often imagine serious reading as something that requires a large protected block: an armchair, an hour, silence, perhaps a cup of coffee. Those conditions are excellent when they appear. They are not necessary for every page.

A novel read twenty minutes at a time is still a novel read. Fifty pages absorbed across several rides are not lesser pages because they arrived between stations.

The arithmetic is the advantage. A commute repeats often enough that modest sessions become large totals without ever feeling large while they happen.

Use a reader that removes reasons not to start

Guten works particularly well for this kind of routine because the book can simply live on the Android device already making the trip. Its Project Gutenberg catalog provides more than 70,000 public-domain books, downloaded books work offline, and no account is required.

That means tunnels, weak service, and underground platforms do not interrupt the reading session if the book is already downloaded.

For driving commutes, Guten Premium adds text-to-speech read-aloud. It is not the same thing as a professionally narrated audiobook, but it gives a practical audio route through the same public-domain text when looking at a screen is impossible. Later, the book is still there to be read normally.

Give the commute a longer project

There is a satisfying difference between filling twenty minutes and using twenty minutes toward something.

Pick a book you have meant to read. Work through a novelist in order. Spend a semester with ancient history. Read the major works of one philosopher. Take on a book long enough that you would normally keep postponing it.

The commute then becomes part of a project with visible progress. Monday's ride is not an isolated pocket of time. It is another twenty pages of Middlemarch, another chapter of Frederick Douglass, another section of Plato.

That is where productivity becomes more than merely being busy. The time leaves something behind.

Protect the first minute

The first minute of the commute often determines the rest of it. Open a feed first and the ride can vanish into fragments. Open the book first and the book has a chance to take over.

Make that first action automatic. Sit down, open the reader. Get into the car, start the prepared audio while parked. Do not ask the commute what it feels like doing today.

Habit formation does not need to be grander than that. The same cue appears every day, and the same response follows it.

Count what repetition gives you

Five hours in a single week may not transform a reading life. Five hours repeated for months can.

The person with a half-hour commute does not need to discover a hidden reservoir of leisure. The reservoir is already visible. It is simply divided into pieces small enough to be mistaken for nothing.

Recapturing those pieces is one of the rare productivity strategies that does not require making the day longer. The travel still happens. The difference is that, at the end of it, more books have been read.
