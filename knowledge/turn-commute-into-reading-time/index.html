How to Turn Your Commute Into Reading Time

A commute is recurring time you already own. Put books into it and let repetition do the work.

Donald

One of the easiest ways to read more is to stop looking for new time. There is probably some time in the day that is already spoken for but not really being used. For millions of people, it is the commute.

A half hour each way, five days a week, is five hours. Over a sixteen-week semester, that is eighty hours. Keep the same schedule for a year and the number is roughly 260. Nobody experiences those hours as a single magnificent block of leisure, of course. They arrive twenty minutes here, thirty-five there, one ordinary Tuesday after another. That is why they are easy to lose.

Reading is unusually well suited to recovering them. You do not need another appointment, another evening, or a heroic weekend plan. You need a book ready when the interval begins.

Do the arithmetic on the time you already have

The value of a commute is repetition. Twenty minutes once is not much. Twenty minutes every weekday can carry you through a novel, then another, then another.

That matters because many reading ambitions fail for a boring reason: they are assigned to imaginary future time. The long book will be started when things calm down. The history will be read over vacation. The classic will wait for an uncluttered Sunday afternoon.

The commute is less romantic and more dependable. It keeps showing up.

Used well, it can become one of the steadiest forms of intellectual work in the week. Books get finished. A subject that once felt unfamiliar becomes familiar through repeated contact. Difficult works are easier to stay inside because the gap between sessions is a day, not two weeks.

Give the commute a default

Short stretches of time are usually claimed by whatever requires the least thought. Open the news. Check messages. Scroll for a while. Look up and discover that the train is three stops farther along.

The simplest way around that is to make reading the default before the commute begins. Not a daily aspiration. A rule.

On the train, sit down and open the book. On the bus, open the book. If the platform wait is ten minutes, that counts too. The important part is the first action. Once something else has captured the interval, reclaiming it is harder.

Driving is different for obvious reasons. Do not read in the car. Prepare the audio before moving, then listen. The underlying habit is the same: the trip starts, and the book starts with it.

Make the book easier to start than everything else

Most commute-reading systems do not fail because the reader lacks ambition. They fail because five minutes of setup consumes a fifteen-minute opportunity.

Download the book in advance. Know what comes next. Set the font size before Monday morning. If you drive, have the text-to-speech or audiobook ready while the car is parked. Keep the reader where it can be opened without hunting through folders.

There is no virtue in making the system elaborate. The whole advantage is that the commute supplies the schedule for free.

This is also why reading on a phone can be so effective despite all the understandable affection for paper. The phone is already coming with you. A paperback left on the kitchen table cannot compete with that.

Use the commute for something large enough to matter

It is tempting to treat small intervals as suitable only for small reading. They are not.

A long novel works. So does biography, history, philosophy, essays, or a project like reading several books by the same writer. In some ways the regularity helps with demanding material. The argument, characters, and vocabulary remain active because you return to them tomorrow.

Choose something you would be pleased to have read six weeks from now. Then let the commute carry the burden a few pages at a time.

There is a satisfying difference between filling twenty minutes and using twenty minutes toward something. Monday's ride becomes another chapter of Middlemarch. Tuesday is twenty more pages of Frederick Douglass. A month of ordinary trips leaves behind a finished book instead of a blur of disconnected screen time.

Reading and listening can serve the same project

Public transit makes ordinary visual reading easy. Driving does not, but that does not mean the reading project has to disappear on driving days.

Guten is useful here because the same public-domain book can live on the Android device already making the trip. The free reader offers more than 70,000 Project Gutenberg books and supports offline reading. Guten Premium adds text-to-speech read-aloud.

Text-to-speech is not a professionally performed audiobook, and it does not need to pretend to be one. Its value is practical. A book that can be read on the train can also be heard in the car, then opened as text again later. The format changes; the project continues.

For anyone using audio while driving, set it up before the car moves and leave the phone alone on the road.

The gain is not the half hour. It is the accumulation.

Productivity advice often asks people to squeeze more activity into an already crowded day. Commute reading is different. The travel is happening anyway.

Nothing has to be added. A piece of time that was previously hard to notice is simply assigned a better use.

Five hours in one week is pleasant. Five hours every week becomes a reading life. Over months, the effect is visible on the shelf of books finished, in the subjects learned, and in the number of works that no longer live on a someday list.

The commute does not have to be dead time. It is recurring time. That is much more valuable.
