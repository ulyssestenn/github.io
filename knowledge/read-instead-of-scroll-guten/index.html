Reading Instead of Scrolling: How to Turn Spare Phone Time Into Book Time

The phone can be the thing that interrupts reading, or the place where a book is always one tap away. The difference is often less about discipline than about what the device makes easiest.

Donald

The most practical way to read more is not to manufacture an extra hour. It is to reclaim some of the small intervals already being handed to a feed. Put a book on the same device, make it easier to open than social media, and use five or ten minutes as real reading time instead of waiting for the mythical quiet evening.

This does not require treating social media as poison. Social networks can be useful, funny, social, informative, and sometimes genuinely important. The target is narrower: the automatic session that begins without a purpose and ends twenty minutes later with very little to show for the attention spent.

Endless scrolling and reading ask different things of attention

A book asks the reader to remain inside one context. A short-form feed is built around repeated novelty: new person, new subject, new soundtrack, new visual frame, new reason to stay for another few seconds.

That difference is not merely aesthetic. A 2025 systematic review and meta-analysis of 71 studies involving 98,299 participants found that heavier short-form-video use was associated with poorer cognitive outcomes overall, with the strongest associations in attention and inhibitory control. The authors were careful about the limits of the evidence: much of the literature is correlational, so it does not prove that short videos caused those differences.

Experimental work offers a more specific clue. Across seven experiments, researchers found that rapidly switching between digital videos in an attempt to avoid boredom could actually increase boredom while reducing attention, satisfaction, and perceived meaning. Another experiment found that unlimited switching among short-form videos impaired performance on a prospective-memory task compared with limited switching or a control condition.

The problem is not that every short video damages the brain. The more defensible point is that a high-switching, endless-choice environment trains a very different pattern of attention from staying with one sustained piece of language.

Reading has modest benefits worth taking seriously

Claims about books can become as inflated as claims about technology. Reading one novel does not make somebody wiser, kinder, or more intelligent by Tuesday.

Still, the evidence is not empty. A 2024 pair of preregistered meta-analyses of fiction reading found small cognitive benefits in experiments, particularly for empathy and mentalizing, and small positive associations between lifetime print-fiction exposure and verbal ability, general cognitive ability, and social cognition. Earlier meta-analysis likewise found a small experimental effect of fiction reading on social cognition.

Small effects are still effects. More importantly, a book is doing something useful with the time even before any measurable cognitive benefit appears: extending attention across an argument or story, adding vocabulary and knowledge, exercising memory for characters and ideas, and delivering an experience that has structure rather than merely duration.

Do not make “less social media” the whole plan

Restriction by itself is an unreliable strategy. In a randomized trial, reducing social-media screen time did not improve objective measures of sustained attention or emotional well-being, even though participants reported feeling more attentive.

That result is useful because it points away from moral bookkeeping. The goal is not to earn virtue points for fewer screen minutes. The goal is to replace a low-value default with a higher-value one.

“Do not scroll” leaves an empty space. “Read the next four pages” supplies an action.

Make the book the easiest icon to tap

Guten is built around Project Gutenberg's catalog of more than 70,000 public-domain books. The useful habit is simple:

  1. Download several books that are genuinely appealing, not merely respectable.
  2. Keep Guten on the first home screen or another easy-to-reach position.
  3. When the hand reaches for a feed from boredom rather than intention, open the current book first.
  4. Read until the available interval ends. Five minutes counts.

The fourth step matters. A reading habit that requires a lamp, a chair, a cup of tea, and forty uninterrupted minutes will lose to a phone that is already in the hand.

Use books that survive interruption

Not every book is equally good for reclaimed fragments of time.

A long nineteenth-century novel can work surprisingly well because chapters create natural stopping points. Essays, short stories, letters, biographies, travel writing, detective fiction, and collections of speeches are even easier to enter and leave.

The public domain is useful here because experimentation costs nothing. If the chosen book turns out to be wrong for a waiting room or ten minutes before an appointment, download another one. There is no reason to force a bad fit merely because it was purchased.

Listening can keep the same book moving

Reading is not practical during every part of the day. Walking, household work, commuting, and other hands-busy periods create another category of otherwise unused book time.

Guten Premium includes text-to-speech read-aloud. That allows a book to move into listening mode when looking at the screen is inconvenient, then back into ordinary visual reading when there is time to sit down with the text again.

This is not a claim that listening and visual reading are identical experiences. They are different modes with different strengths. The useful self-improvement trick is continuity: the book does not have to disappear from the day merely because the eyes are occupied.

A difficult paragraph may deserve visual rereading. Dialogue may be delightful aloud. Dense notes may be easier on the page. The format can follow the situation instead of dictating whether the book gets encountered at all.

Build a rule around intent, not guilt

A workable rule might be:

Before opening a feed with no specific purpose, read two pages.

Or:

Keep one book in progress and use it for the first ten minutes of every idle-phone interval.

These rules are small enough to survive an ordinary day. They also avoid pretending that all social-media use is equivalent. Opening a message from a friend is not the same behavior as falling into an algorithmic feed because the elevator is taking twelve seconds.

Measure the outcome that matters

There is little value in turning this into another elaborate habit tracker. The useful outcome is not a streak. It is books actually read.

After a month, ask a few plain questions:

  • Were more books finished?
  • Did reading become easier to begin?
  • Did idle scrolling become more intentional rather than automatic?
  • Are there books, ideas, or passages now present in memory that would otherwise have been another sequence of forgotten posts?

If the answer is yes, the system is working.

The phone is not the enemy of reading

The same device that contains the infinite feed can contain Austen, Douglass, Melville, Woolf, Du Bois, Shakespeare, Chekhov, Dickens, ancient texts in older translations, and tens of thousands of stranger things.

The important difference is not paper versus pixels. It is whether the next tap begins an intentional encounter with something that has depth, or another sequence designed to make the next tap easier.

Read more. Scroll less. Not because every minute has to be productive, but because attention is finite and a book can give something back for it.
