How to Search a DVD or Blu-ray Collection by Spine Text

Shelf Scan does not care whether the text belongs to a book. If a movie case has readable words on the spine, those words can become searchable.

Sasha

A large movie shelf develops the same problem as a large bookcase: the collection is visually present but not necessarily retrievable. You may know you own a film without remembering which shelf it is on, whether it is filed under the director, the title, a box set, or some private logic that made sense three years ago.

Shelf Scan uses optical character recognition on visible shelf text. That means DVDs and Blu-rays are not a special case. The app is looking for words, not ISBN records.

Use the title when the title is distinctive

In Live Mode, enter a useful word from the film title and point the camera across the cases. Matches are highlighted in the camera view, and the optional match tone can help when you are moving along a long shelf.

A distinctive word usually works better than a common one. Searching Leopard is more useful than searching The. If the title is tiny but a director or collection name is prominent, search the text that is actually visible.

Saved shelf photos work too

If the collection is stable, photograph the shelves once and save the images to Shelf Scan's Library. The recognized text from those images can then be searched later without standing in front of the collection.

This is useful for movie collections in cabinets, media rooms, closets, or several different rooms. A simple description such as media room west case is often enough location information.

Box sets need the words the box actually shows

A collection may contain a film inside a director set, franchise box, television season set, or boutique-label collection. The individual title you remember may not appear on the outward-facing spine.

Search cannot retrieve text that is not visible. If the shelf says Three Films by... rather than listing every title, search the box-set wording or the director's name. The photograph itself still provides useful visual context once you find the right region.

Reflective cases and stylized logos can be harder

Movie packaging often gives OCR less friendly material than ordinary book spines: glossy plastic, metallic printing, narrow cases, vertical type, logos, and low-contrast artwork. A steadier camera and soft light help. For saved Library images, a few closer photographs are generally more useful than one wide picture of an entire wall.

If one title refuses to scan, that does not invalidate the shelf. OCR is a retrieval aid, not a perfect inventory.

You do not need to catalog the collection first

A traditional movie database can track edition, format, runtime, region, director, cast, and purchase history. Those fields are useful if you need them. They are unnecessary if the recurring question is simply, “Where is the movie I already own?”

For that narrower problem, the spine may already contain enough metadata. Photograph it or point the camera at it, then search the words that are there.

The tool used in this workflow

Shelf Scan

Shelf Scan uses on-device OCR to search visible text on physical shelves. Live Mode highlights matches through the camera, while the Library saves shelf photos and searches recognized text across them. The same approach works for books, movies, games, and other collections with readable spine text.
