Homer is unusually easy to turn into a handsome quotation card and unusually easy to damage by doing so.

The epics are full of sentences that sound complete when isolated but depend on a speaker, a simile, an argument, or an action established just before the selected lines. A beautiful image can make the extraction feel more authoritative than it is. The problem is not quotation itself. The problem is making the crop cleaner than the meaning.

Breaker of Horses can turn a selected passage from the *Iliad* or *Odyssey* into a formatted image. Before choosing the visual style, it is worth deciding what the quotation needs in order to remain honest.

## Start one sentence earlier than you think you need

A quotation often becomes misleading because the first visible pronoun has lost its noun.

`He answered...` is not much use if the image does not identify who `he` is. A line beginning `And then...` may depend entirely on the action just above it. Homeric speeches can also move quickly between narrator, speaker, quoted speech inside the speech, and simile.

When a passage catches your attention, expand the selection backward until the reader can answer the basic questions without guessing:

- Who is speaking?
- Who or what is being described?
- What action or claim is this sentence responding to?

You do not always need the entire scene. Often one preceding clause is enough.

The same rule applies at the end. If the selected sentence appears to reach a moral conclusion but the next line immediately complicates it, the neat image may be doing violence to the passage.

## Keep the translation attached to the words

`Homer said` is not quite enough when the English words belong to Samuel Butler, Alexander Pope, George Chapman, or another translator.

Those translators are not interchangeable delivery systems. Butler's prose can make an action plain where Pope's heroic couplets reorganize it into eighteenth-century English verse. Chapman may choose another rhythm, vocabulary, or emphasis again.

If you share an English quotation, identify the translator when practical. For example:

> *Odyssey*, Book 8, trans. Samuel Butler

or

> Homer, *Iliad* 1, trans. Alexander Pope

This tells the reader whose English sentences are on the image and makes it possible to find the passage again.

If you are quoting the Greek, Greek line numbers can be useful coordinates. They are especially helpful when the corresponding English translations divide or reorder sentences differently.

## Do not make three translations look like one quotation

Side-by-side reading creates a particular temptation: combine your favorite wording from one translation with a phrase from another because the result sounds excellent.

At that point you have created a new composite text.

There is nothing inherently wrong with discussing several renderings together, but the image should make the distinction visible. Put the translations in separate blocks or make separate images. Do not present a stitched sentence under Homer's name as though it were a historical translation.

The differences are part of the reason to compare them.

## Similes need more room than aphorisms

Homeric similes often open into another world for several lines before returning to the battlefield or voyage. Extracting only the most quotable sentence from the middle can make the passage sound like a freestanding maxim when it is actually doing comparative work.

The famous simile in *Odyssey* 8, for example, compares Odysseus' weeping to the grief of a woman whose husband has fallen defending his city. The force of the comparison lies in the relation between those figures. Reducing it to a generic line about sorrow removes the disturbing asymmetry that makes the simile worth reading.

When the passage is interesting because of a comparison, preserve enough of both sides of the comparison for the reader to see it.

This may produce a longer image. That is fine. A quotation image is not obliged to become a slogan.

## Preserve ambiguity when the poem preserves ambiguity

Another kind of bad crop resolves something the text leaves open.

An epic can describe an action without explaining a motive, or place two ideas beside one another without telling the reader exactly how to reconcile them. A caption that announces `Homer's lesson is...` may be more aggressive than the passage itself.

The image can simply present the lines.

If you want to make an interpretive claim, put that claim in the accompanying text and distinguish it from the quotation. This gives the reader a chance to disagree with the interpretation without first having to untangle it from Homer's words.

## Let the source line do useful work

A good source line is compact but recoverable. At minimum, include the work and book number. Add the translator for English.

Useful:

`Homer, Odyssey 9 · Samuel Butler translation`

Less useful:

`Homer`

A reader who wants the larger scene should not have to search the entire epic from a sentence fragment.

If the image is going into a classroom, article, or research context, use the citation convention appropriate to that setting rather than assuming a social-media card is a scholarly citation.

## Choose the visual style last

Once the selection is sound, the design can do what design is good at: make the passage inviting to read.

Breaker of Horses offers several visual formats for selected passages and can save the resulting image to the device. The visual treatment should serve the words rather than compete with them. A long, syntactically dense passage benefits from generous line spacing and a quiet background. A four-line exchange can tolerate more visual drama.

The mistake is deciding first that you want a square card with 28 words and then cutting the passage until it fits.

Choose the text that deserves to survive extraction. Then choose the image that can hold it.
