The quotation is usually the easiest part to save. The harder question appears six months later: `Where did this come from, and why did I care?`

A useful clip preserves more than content. It preserves enough provenance and working context that the saved text can still be interpreted, checked, and used later.

Keep Clip can receive text and links through Android Share and capture metadata such as source app, title, and URL when the source provides it. That is a good starting point. It is not always the whole record you need.

## The minimum useful clip has content plus a path back

For a web passage, the URL is often the most valuable piece of metadata after the quotation itself.

The quote tells you what caught your attention. The link lets you:

- read the surrounding argument
- check whether the wording changed
- identify the author and publication date
- verify that your remembered interpretation still matches the source
- cite the original rather than citing your own clipping

Without the link, the clip can become a detached sentence that looks authoritative because you saved it.

This is especially dangerous with claims, statistics, and quotations. A sentence can be accurate in context and misleading outside it.

## Save the title when URLs are ugly or unstable

A URL is excellent for machines and often terrible for human memory.

`/article/2025/08/14?id=92817` does not tell you much when it appears in a search result. A clear title gives the source a recognizable identity even if the link later breaks.

When Keep Clip receives a page title automatically, keep it unless it is obviously useless. If the sharing app supplies no meaningful title, a short custom clip name can make later retrieval much easier.

The title does not have to reproduce the site's headline exactly if you are using a private label for retrieval, but do not rename a source in a way that makes your interpretation look like the source's own title.

A useful pattern is:

`Source title — your short context`

or simply keep the original title and put your comment in the note field.

## Add why it mattered when that is not obvious from the quotation

Some clips explain themselves.

A recipe link saved because you want the recipe probably does not need `Reason: recipe`.

A research passage often does.

Short notes can preserve the missing relationship:

- `Supports claim about library search behavior.`
- `Counterexample to the argument in draft 3.`
- `Check the original study before citing.`
- `Compare this translation with Pope.`
- `Possible source for chapter on data portability.`

This is not permanent annotation. It is an aid to your future self, who will not share the exact mental context of the capture moment.

## Distinguish the source's words from your words

When a clip contains a quotation and your reaction, make the boundary visible.

For example:

**Clip:** the selected source text  
**Note:** `This seems to conflict with the 2024 paper.`

Do not edit the quotation itself to insert your interpretation unless the clip is clearly marked as a paraphrase.

The difference matters because saved material often migrates. A note copied into a draft or exported to Markdown can lose visual cues that were obvious in the original app. Keeping quotation and commentary structurally distinct makes that migration safer.

## For books, preserve edition or translation when wording matters

A book title and author may not be enough.

For ancient works, classics, translated literature, and heavily revised editions, the English wording belongs to a particular translator or edition. If the passage may be quoted later, save that information while it is easy to see.

Likewise, page numbers are useful only if they correspond to a stable edition. An ebook location or chapter heading may be a better recovery clue in some contexts.

A private clip does not need a complete Chicago-style citation on arrival. It does need enough information that a proper citation can be reconstructed without guessing.

## For links, save enough of the claim to survive link rot

The opposite problem also exists: keeping only the URL.

A browser bookmark called `interesting article` is almost no archive at all. If the page disappears, you may not remember what information was on it.

When the source matters, save the key excerpt or a short note describing what the page contributed.

This creates a modest amount of resilience:

- the link provides the path back while it works
- the title identifies the source
- the saved excerpt preserves the specific thing you found there
- your note preserves why it entered the collection

That is far more useful than either a naked quote or a naked URL.

It is not the same as web archiving. If preserving the entire page matters, use an archival tool designed for that job.

## Metadata should earn its maintenance cost

It is possible to turn every clip into a miniature library record with author, publication, date, tags, topic, project, status, confidence, archive URL, and twelve other fields.

Most clips do not need that.

Add metadata according to the cost of losing the context.

A disposable shopping link may need nothing beyond the URL. A quotation you expect to publish later deserves source and edition information. A research claim that could support an important argument deserves a clear route back to the original evidence.

The purpose of metadata is not to make the archive look organized. It is to keep the saved thing usable.
