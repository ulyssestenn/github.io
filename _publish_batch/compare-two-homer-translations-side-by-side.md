# How to Compare Two Homer Translations Side by Side

**Author:** Nico
**Format:** Knowledge Guide
**Status:** published
**Product:** Breaker of Horses
**Deck:** Turn on Two texts, choose an English translation for each pane, and decide whether the panes should move together or keep independent positions.

Translation becomes much less abstract when two versions of the same passage are visible at once.

Breaker of Horses includes Samuel Butler's prose, Alexander Pope's verse, and George Chapman's verse for both epics. You can put any two texts beside one another without changing books or trying to relocate the passage in a second reader.

## Choose the book and the first translation

Open *The Iliad* or *The Odyssey* and use the **Book** selector to choose the book number you want.

Under **Primary text**, choose the first translation. The menu identifies the English options as:

- Samuel Butler · prose
- Alexander Pope · verse
- George Chapman · verse

The app also includes Homeric Greek, but you do not need Greek for an English-to-English comparison.

## Turn on Two texts

Switch on **Two texts**.

A **Second text** selector appears. Choose a different translation there.

The reading area now shows both versions at once. This is particularly useful at passages where a difference in form is obvious: a speech, an epithet, a simile, or a sentence whose emphasis seems to change from one translator to another.

## Decide whether the panes should move together

Tap **Aa** to open Reading settings.

When two texts are visible, the settings include a pane-synchronization control. With **Synchronized panes** on, either pane can lead and the other follows the same relative part of the book. Switch it off for **Independent panes** when you want each translation to keep its own position.

Synchronized does not mean exact line-for-line equivalence.

Butler writes prose. Pope and Chapman write verse, and all three divide and expand the source differently. Breaker therefore synchronizes by relative reading position so the corresponding region stays in view; it does not claim that the top line in one pane is a semantic twin of the top line in the other.

## Compare a passage before comparing whole translators

A useful first comparison is small.

Read one passage in the primary pane, then the same stretch in the second. Notice what changes: sentence shape, pace, explicitness, repeated wording, metaphor, register, or where the verse presses hardest.

Butler can be helpful when you want the narrative movement stated in prose. Pope and Chapman make different poetic decisions. None of that means one short passage settles which translation is “best.”

The side-by-side view is more interesting when it turns a vague impression into something visible in the words.

## Switch translations when a third version would clarify the choice

Use **Primary text** or **Second text** again when you want to bring the third English translation into the comparison.

With synchronized panes enabled, the comparison view aligns the second pane to the primary pane's relative reading position when the texts are loaded. Because each edition also has its own saved reading position, it is still worth glancing at the surrounding names and action before making a close comparison after a source change.

For *The Odyssey*, the app credits Pope's translation as Alexander Pope with collaborators. The labels in the reader keep the source identity visible while you compare.

## Product connection
**Breaker of Horses.** Breaker of Horses contains the complete *Iliad* and *Odyssey* in Homeric Greek plus the public-domain English translations of Samuel Butler, Alexander Pope, and George Chapman. Its Two texts mode supports side-by-side reading with synchronized or independent panes.
